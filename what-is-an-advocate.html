Understanding the role

What is a patient advocate?

A patient advocate is a trained professional you hire to stand beside you inside the healthcare system — explaining, organizing, questioning, and making sure decisions get made with you rather than around you.

The short version

Think of a patient advocate the way you'd think of an accountant at tax time or a realtor when you're buying a house. The system is navigable — but it has its own language, its own timelines, and its own rules that nobody hands you in writing. An advocate is someone who already knows those rules and works only for you.

An independent advocate is not employed by a hospital, clinic, insurance company, or care facility. Many hospitals have a “patient advocate” or ombudsman on staff, and they can be genuinely helpful — but they are paid by the institution. When your interests and the institution's interests diverge, an independent advocate has no conflict to manage.

The distinction that matters I am a nurse advocate. Twenty-four years of clinical practice means I can read the chart, understand the medication list, recognize when something in a care plan doesn't add up, and ask a physician a clinical question in the language they use with each other.

What a patient advocate does

  • Attends appointments with you, in person or virtually, and takes detailed notes
  • Prepares the questions you'd otherwise think of in the parking lot afterward
  • Translates diagnoses, test results, and treatment options into plain language
  • Gathers and organizes medical records scattered across systems
  • Coordinates between specialists who aren't talking to each other
  • Reviews bills and Explanation of Benefits statements for errors
  • Writes and files insurance appeals for denied claims and authorizations
  • Advocates at the bedside during a hospital stay and pushes for a safe discharge
  • Researches and vets assisted living, memory care, and nursing home options
  • Keeps distant family members informed and included

What a patient advocate does not do

  • Diagnose conditions or prescribe medication
  • Provide hands-on nursing or personal care in your home
  • Replace your physician, nurse practitioner, or care team
  • Give legal advice or represent you in court
  • Make medical decisions for you — the decisions stay yours
  • Guarantee a specific medical, billing, or insurance outcome
  • Accept payment, referral fees, or commissions from any provider or facility

Where something falls outside my scope, I'll say so plainly and, where I can, point you toward the right professional.

Timing

When it's worth calling an advocate

You don't need to be in a crisis. Most people call at one of these moments.

A serious new diagnosis

Cancer, heart failure, dementia, an autoimmune condition — anything where the treatment path has real branches and the choices are yours to make.

An unplanned hospital stay

Especially when discharge is being discussed and the plan sounds faster than it sounds safe.

A denial or a bill that's wrong

Prior authorizations, out-of-network surprises, duplicate charges, or an appeal with a deadline attached.

Caring from a distance

You live out of state and need eyes, ears, and a nurse's judgment in the room.

A move to a care facility

Assisted living, memory care, or skilled nursing — a decision most families make once, under time pressure.

Nobody is coordinating

Four specialists, three portals, two pharmacies, and no single person holding the whole picture.

Common questions

Questions people ask first

Isn't this what the hospital's case manager is for?

Case managers and discharge planners do important work, and I collaborate with them respectfully. The difference is who employs them. Their role includes managing length of stay and institutional priorities alongside your care. I have one client in the room: you.

Will my doctor be offended if I bring an advocate?

In my experience, the opposite. Clinicians generally welcome a prepared patient with organized records and clear questions — it makes a fifteen-minute appointment far more productive. I'm there to support the relationship with your care team, not to fight it.

Does insurance or Medicare pay for a patient advocate?

Generally, no. Independent advocacy is a private-pay service. Some people use HSA or FSA funds — check with your plan administrator, as eligibility varies. Many families find the cost is offset by billing errors caught or denials overturned, but I never promise that as an outcome.

What does “Board-Certified” actually mean?

The BCPA credential is awarded by the Patient Advocate Certification Board, an independent body. It requires documented professional experience, a qualifying education background, passing a national examination, and adherence to a published code of ethics with ongoing continuing education. It is currently the only nationally recognized certification for the profession.

Do you have to be local to work with you?

No. I attend appointments and hospital visits in person throughout the Bismarck–Mandan area and travel elsewhere in North Dakota by arrangement. Records review, insurance appeals, research, and family coordination all work well virtually, so I support clients across the state and their families anywhere.

Is what I tell you confidential?

Yes. Your information is kept confidential and is shared only with the people and organizations you specifically authorize in writing. That authorization is part of the paperwork we complete before any work begins.

Still not sure whether you need an advocate?

That's exactly what the free call is for. If your situation doesn't call for paid help, I'll tell you that — and point you somewhere useful instead.
